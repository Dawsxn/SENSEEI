3.2  Cognitive Offloading

Cognitive offloading is the act of delegating a mental task to something outside the mind. Writing a phone number on a slip of paper, setting an alarm instead of remembering an appointment, or letting a spreadsheet hold a running total are all instances of the same move: work that could be done internally is handed to an external tool.

The appeal is obvious. Working memory is narrow and easily disrupted, and offloading frees it for whatever the person considers the real task. A navigator who no longer has to hold a route in mind can attend to traffic instead. In this sense offloading is not a failure of memory but a sensible allocation of a scarce resource.

The cost appears later, and it is easy to miss. Retrieving information from memory is itself what strengthens the memory, so a mind that never retrieves never consolidates.

This is why the same behaviour can be either efficient or corrosive depending on what is being offloaded and why. Delegating a total you will never need again costs nothing. Delegating the reasoning you were meant to do yourself costs the understanding you were meant to build.
