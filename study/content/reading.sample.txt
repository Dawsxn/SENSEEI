Switching Costs

A switching cost is whatever a customer gives up in order to move from one
supplier to another. The price of the product is not part of it. What counts is
everything that sits around the purchase: the time spent learning a new system,
the data that has to be moved, the contract that has to be broken, the staff who
have to be retrained, and the risk that the new supplier turns out to be worse
than the old one.

Switching costs matter because they change what a price means. Two suppliers may
charge the same, and a customer may still stay with the more expensive one,
because leaving would cost more than the difference. A firm that has built up
switching costs has bought itself room to raise prices, tolerate a slower
product, or lose a feature comparison, without losing the customer immediately.
The customer's inertia is doing work that the product itself is not.

The costs are not always financial, and the ones that hold customers most firmly
often are not. A team that has spent two years learning a piece of software has
accumulated habits, shortcuts, and workarounds that do not transfer. A hospital
that has recorded a decade of patient histories in one system faces a migration
whose true cost is not the invoice but the chance of losing something. In both
cases the customer is held less by a contract than by the accumulated fit
between themselves and what they already use.

Switching costs can be built deliberately. Long contracts, proprietary formats,
loyalty schemes, and deep integration into a customer's own processes all raise
the cost of leaving. They can also arise without anyone intending them, simply
because a customer has used something long enough to become good at it.

The strategic consequence runs in both directions. A firm protected by high
switching costs is insulated from competitors, but it is equally shut out of
rivals' customers, who face the same barrier in reverse. This is why an incumbent
with loyal customers can be slow to grow: the market it would need to take is
held by the same force that holds its own.
